# Mr. I: perceptual, imagined, remembered, dreamed, and musical color all disappear

## Account

After an accident at 65, the painter Jonathan I. abruptly lost the construction of color throughout conscious life. The world was not merely desaturated like a pleasant black-and-white film. It looked wrong, dirty, leaden, harshly contrasted, and at times visually unintelligible. Reds could appear black; yellows and blues nearly white; low-contrast objects could disappear while silhouettes became extraordinarily sharp.

The change crossed the perception/imagery boundary. Mr. I.'s previously vivid imagery remained detailed but became colorless, so closing his eyes did not restore the expected tomato or human flesh. Dreams became washed out or violently contrasty. Migraine geometry and pressure-induced flashes lost color. Music also became impoverished because his previously automatic tone-to-color synesthesia vanished.

Color knowledge survived. He could state precise names and Pantone numbers for familiar colors and describe colors in known paintings, yet neither visible wavelength patterns nor memory could yield the phenomenal quality.

## Immediate course

The accident included transient amnesia and several days of alexia: sharply focused print initially looked like an unknown script. The next day, driving seemed foggy and bleached; he passed red lights without recognizing them by color. In his studio his own brightly colored paintings appeared gray and lost their intelligibility.

## Structure of the experience

- **Perceptual color:** wholly absent after a lifetime of normal color vision.
- **Imagined and remembered color:** absent despite preserved vivid form imagery.
- **Dream and hallucinatory color:** absent.
- **Semantic color knowledge:** unusually precise and preserved.
- **Wavelength/tonal discrimination:** enough information survived to sort yarn rapidly into gray-value bands, but it did not become color.
- **Contrast/form:** sometimes enhanced as silhouette, sometimes lost at low contrast.
- **Cross-modal color:** musical concurrents disappeared with visual color.

## Functional consequences and adaptation

Human flesh looked abhorrent; social and sexual life contracted. Colored food became disgusting even with eyes closed, so he turned to black-and-white foods. Clothes were labeled and sorted, and traffic lights identified by position. Museums and old paintings became painful reminders of a lost identity.

He built an all-gray room to communicate the 360-degree solidity of the new world. After failed attempts to paint in unseen color, he moved to black-and-white painting and sculpture, working up to 18 hours a day. The high-contrast world eventually generated a new visual style and renewed artistic life, though not restored color.

## Key contrasts

`knows exact color` / `cannot experience color`  
`form imagery vivid` / `imagined color absent`  
`music heard` / `automatic musical color absent`  
`fine color distinctions lost` / `some tonal and silhouette distinctions enhanced`

## Why this case matters

Mr. I. shows that color is not one field. Perceptual color, imagery, memory, dream color, migraine color, semantic knowledge, wavelength discrimination, affective meaning, and synesthetic color can change together or remain separate in a highly structured pattern.
