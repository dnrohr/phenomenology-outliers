# Ian Waterman: movement rebuilt through vision after loss of body-position sense

## Account

At 19, Ian Waterman developed a rare neurological illness that removed touch and proprioceptive sensation below the neck. Without continuous information about joint position, muscle state, and bodily placement, he initially collapsed and could neither stand nor control his limbs in the automatic way he had before.

There was no neurological recovery. Waterman instead rebuilt movement as a consciously supervised visual task. To know where a limb was, he had to see it. Actions normally launched and corrected beneath awareness became sequences that had to be planned, watched, and continually adjusted.

## Course and adaptation

After months in a neurological ward he was judged unlikely to walk again. Through prolonged practice he developed visual routines for posture, balance, reaching, grasping, dressing, eating, and eventually walking. He learned to preconfigure his body, keep relevant limbs in view, and substitute visual geometry and explicit intention for absent peripheral feedback.

The achievement did not restore automatic embodiment. Ordinary mobility remained a daily cognitive marathon: divided attention, darkness, closing the eyes, or losing the body from view could remove the substitute control channel. The book follows these consequences into work, intimacy, and marriage rather than treating laboratory movement as the whole case.

## Structure of the experience

- **Proprioceptive presence:** absent below the neck.
- **Motor pathways and strength:** sufficiently preserved for movement.
- **Automatic control:** devastated when deprived of feedback.
- **Visual substitution:** body position can be inferred from sight.
- **Attention:** movement requires sustained conscious supervision.
- **Body as lived vs seen object:** the body must become a visually monitored object in order to be controlled.

## Key contrasts

`motor command available` / `felt limb position absent`  
`movement under vision possible` / `movement without vision precarious`  
`neurological sensation not restored` / `functional ability extensively relearned`

## Why this case matters

Waterman's case makes usually invisible bodily background information phenomenologically explicit by its loss. It separates willing a movement, executing it, sensing it, seeing it, and automatically correcting it.
